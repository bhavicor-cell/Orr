The Situation

A founder running operations across multiple ventures under shared capital exposure. Revenue was coming in — deals were closing, invoices were going out — but cash in the bank consistently fell short of what had been earned. The gap was real. Nobody had identified the cause.

On paper the business looked healthy. In practice refunds were being processed without a clear approval path, inventory costs were distributed across ventures with no consolidated view, and cash collection was happening when someone remembered to follow up — not on a schedule.

Nobody had built the system that moved money from sale to bank account reliably. The founder had assumed it was running. It wasn't.

The Diagnosis

Three separate failure points were operating simultaneously — each one independently manageable, together significant.

  • Refund processing had no defined path. Requests arrived informally, were approved inconsistently, and were processed without tracking. Refund volume was unknown until month-end reconciliation.
  • Inventory costs were recorded per venture but never consolidated. The actual cost of goods sold across the group was an estimate. Capital allocation decisions were being made on incomplete data.
  • Cash collection relied on manual follow-up. Follow-ups happened when a team member noticed a gap — not on a schedule. Late payments drifted. Disputes surfaced late.
What Was Built

Three systems were installed in sequence, starting with cash collection as the highest-leverage fix.

  • An Order-to-Cash cadence with defined invoice timing, a structured follow-up sequence at 7, 14, and 21 days, exception handling for disputes, and a named owner for each stage.
  • A refund governance process with a single intake path, approval criteria, and a weekly review log. Refund volume became visible and trackable for the first time.
  • A consolidated inventory and cost model across all ventures, built to be maintained weekly rather than reconciled quarterly.

The fix wasn't complicated. It was just never built. Three systems, installed in sequence, and the gap closed.

What Changed

Within 90 days of installation, $150K in margin leakage had been recovered. The cash cycle moved from ad-hoc to a defined 7–21 day collection sequence. Refund volume became visible — the founder had a number for the first time. Capital decisions across all ventures moved from estimate-based to current-data-based.

The engagement closed when the systems were handed over, documented, and running without ORR in the loop. That's how every sprint ends.
