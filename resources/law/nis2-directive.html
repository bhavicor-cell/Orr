Chapter IGeneral Provisions
Art. 1

Subject Matter

This Directive sets measures to achieve a high common level of cybersecurity across the EU. It establishes: obligations for member states to adopt national cybersecurity strategies and designate competent authorities and CSIRTs; cybersecurity risk-management and incident-reporting obligations for essential and important entities; rules on cybersecurity information sharing; and supervisory and enforcement obligations for member states.

Art. 2

Scope

This Directive applies to public or private entities that: qualify as medium-sized enterprises or larger under EU size criteria and provide services or carry out activities within the EU in sectors listed in Annex I (essential) or Annex II (important); or fall into specific categories regardless of size — including providers of public electronic communications networks or services, trust service providers, top-level domain name registries, DNS service providers, sole providers of a service essential for social or economic activities in a member state, entities whose disruption could have significant systemic impact, entities identified as critical under the Critical Entities Resilience Directive (EU) 2022/2557, and entities that member states identified under the previous NIS Directive.

It does not apply to entities in national security, public security, defence, or law enforcement — including the prevention, investigation, detection, and prosecution of criminal offences. It does not apply to parliaments, central banks, or the judiciary. Member states may exempt specific entities engaged in national security, defence, or law enforcement activities.

Where sector-specific EU legislation imposes cybersecurity risk-management or incident-reporting obligations that are at least equivalent to those in this Directive, those sector-specific rules take precedence for the entities covered by them.

Art. 3

Essential and Important Entities

Essential entities are: entities in Annex I sectors that exceed the medium-sized enterprise ceiling; qualified trust service providers, top-level domain registries, DNS service providers, and public electronic communications network or service providers regardless of size; entities designated by member states; entities identified as critical under Directive (EU) 2022/2557; and entities previously identified under the first NIS Directive where the member state decides to retain that designation.

Important entities are all entities in Annex I or II sectors that fall within scope but are not classified as essential. Member states must establish a list of essential and important entities (and entities providing domain registration services) by 17 April 2025, and update it regularly.

Art. 4–5

Sector-Specific Acts and Minimum Harmonisation

Where a sector-specific EU act requires cybersecurity risk management or incident reporting with effects at least equivalent to this Directive, the sector-specific act applies instead for those aspects. The Commission maintains and publishes guidance on how equivalence is assessed.

This Directive does not prevent member states from adopting or maintaining higher levels of cybersecurity, provided they are consistent with EU law.

Art. 6

Definitions — selected key terms

Network and information system — an electronic communications network; any device or group of interconnected devices that process digital data by running a program; or digital data stored, processed, retrieved, or transmitted by the above.

Security of network and information systems — the ability of those systems to resist, at a given level of confidence, any event that compromises the availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of stored, transmitted, or processed data, or of the services offered through those systems.

Incident — an event compromising the availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of stored, transmitted, or processed data, or of the services offered by or accessible via network and information systems.

Significant incident — an incident that has caused or is capable of causing severe operational disruption of the services or financial loss to the entity, or has affected or is capable of affecting other natural or legal persons by causing considerable material or non-material damage.

Large-scale cybersecurity incident — an incident causing a level of disruption that exceeds a member state's capacity to respond to it, or that has significant impact in at least two member states.

Near miss — an event that could have compromised the availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of data or services, but was successfully prevented or did not materialise.

Cyber threat — any potential circumstance, event, or action that could damage, disrupt, or otherwise adversely impact network and information systems, their users, or other persons.

CSIRT — Computer Security Incident Response Team, designated or established under Article 10.

Vulnerability — a weakness, susceptibility, or flaw of an ICT product or service that can be exploited by a cyber threat.

Chapter IICoordinated Cybersecurity Frameworks
Art. 7

National Cybersecurity Strategy

Each member state must adopt a national cybersecurity strategy setting out strategic objectives, the resources needed, policy and regulatory measures to achieve them, and a governance framework clarifying roles and responsibilities. The strategy must address: supply-chain security; vulnerability management; promotion of cybersecurity in education; promotion of cybersecurity tools and technology including AI; promotion of academic research; cyber hygiene and awareness; and support for SMEs. Strategies must be assessed at least every five years and updated where necessary.

Art. 8

Competent Authorities and Single Points of Contact

Each member state must designate one or more competent authorities to supervise this Directive's implementation. Each member state must also designate a single point of contact to liaise with authorities in other member states, with the Cooperation Group, and with the CSIRT network. The single point of contact must be given adequate resources. If a member state designates more than one competent authority, one must coordinate supervision and cross-border cooperation.

Art. 9

National Cyber Crisis Management Frameworks

Each member state must designate one or more authorities responsible for managing large-scale cybersecurity incidents and crises. Member states must adopt a national large-scale cybersecurity incident and crisis response plan setting out: preparedness objectives and measures; roles and responsibilities; procedures for crisis management, including integration into the general national crisis management framework; and communication channels. These plans must be tested through regular exercises.

Art. 10–11

CSIRTs

Each member state must designate or establish one or more CSIRTs covering at least the sectors and entity types in Annexes I and II. CSIRTs must have adequate resources, a clearly defined mandate, and the technical capability to carry out their tasks. Their tasks include: monitoring and analysing cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and incidents; providing early warnings, alerts, announcements, and information dissemination; responding to incidents and providing assistance; collecting and analysing forensic data; performing proactive scanning (with consent or when authorised by law); and contributing to the deployment of secure information-sharing tools.

CSIRTs must be able to communicate securely. They must establish cooperation relationships with relevant private-sector entities. They must have well-maintained infrastructure including redundant systems and secure communication. Member states must notify the Commission of the CSIRT's mandate and incident-handling process.

Art. 12

Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure and European Vulnerability Database

Each member state must designate a CSIRT as coordinator for coordinated vulnerability disclosure. ENISA must develop and maintain a European vulnerability database (EUVD) containing: a description of vulnerabilities; affected ICT products or services and their severity; availability of patches; and relevant information on exploitation. Anyone — entities, manufacturers, CSIRTs, or individuals — may report a vulnerability to the coordinator CSIRT. The coordinator must ensure that the entity concerned is contacted, given a reasonable deadline to patch, and that a fix is coordinated before voluntary public disclosure. The coordinator CSIRT may also set its own disclosure timelines where it considers the risk warrants it.

Chapter IIICooperation
Art. 13–14

Cooperation Group and CSIRTs Network

The Cooperation Group is established to support and facilitate strategic cooperation and information exchange among member states, and to strengthen trust and confidence. It is composed of representatives of member states, the Commission, and ENISA (observer). It provides strategic guidance, coordinates vulnerability disclosure, discusses national strategies, exchanges best practices on supervision and enforcement, and develops a methodology for peer reviews. The Group meets at least twice a year and produces biennial reports.

The CSIRTs network is composed of member state CSIRTs and CERT-EU (observer), with ENISA providing the secretariat. Its tasks include: exchanging technical information on incidents, vulnerabilities, tools, and cyber threats; exchanging best practices; facilitating mutual assistance; cooperating with the private sector; discussing incident response capabilities; contributing to coordinated responses; and conducting exercises.

Art. 15–16

EU-CyCLONe

EU-CyCLONe (European Cyber Crises Liaison Organisation Network) is established to support the coordinated management of large-scale cybersecurity incidents and crises at the operational level. It is composed of representatives of member state cyber crisis management authorities and the Commission. Its tasks include: increasing preparedness for large-scale incidents and crises; developing a shared situational awareness; assessing consequences and impact; coordinating management among member states; and discussing national crisis response plans. EU-CyCLONe reports to the European Parliament and Council.

Art. 17–19

Peer Reviews and ENISA

Voluntary peer reviews among member states may be established to assess national cybersecurity capabilities and identify best practices and potential improvements. Peer-review experts must have relevant experience and must not come from the member state under review. Results are shared with the reviewed member state and the Cooperation Group, and summaries are made publicly available.

ENISA issues a biennial report on the state of cybersecurity in the EU, including a statistical overview. ENISA also supports the Cooperation Group, the CSIRTs network, and EU-CyCLONe in carrying out their functions and in facilitating cooperation.

Chapter IVCybersecurity Risk Management and Reporting
Art. 20

Governance

The management bodies of essential and important entities must approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures taken by the entity, oversee their implementation, and can be held liable for infringements. Members of management bodies must undergo training, and must encourage entities to offer similar training to their employees, to acquire sufficient knowledge and skills to identify risks and assess cybersecurity risk-management practices and their impact on the entity's services.

Art. 21

Cybersecurity Risk-Management Measures

Essential and important entities must take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational, and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of their network and information systems. Measures must be based on an all-hazards approach, protect against incidents, and be proportionate to the risk, the entity's size, likelihood and severity of incidents, and economic and societal impact.

At a minimum, the measures must include: policies on risk analysis and information-system security; incident handling; business continuity, backup management, disaster recovery, and crisis management; supply-chain security, including security aspects concerning relationships with direct suppliers and service providers; security in network and information system acquisition, development, and maintenance, including vulnerability handling and disclosure; policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk-management measures; basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training; policies on the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption; human resources security, access control policies, and asset management; and the use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication, secured voice, video, and text communications, and secured emergency communication systems within the entity.

When assessing proportionality, account must be taken of the entity's degree of exposure to risks, its size, the likelihood and severity of incidents, and any cross-border impact. The Commission may adopt implementing acts specifying the technical and methodological requirements of these measures for specific types of entities.

Art. 22

Union-Level Coordinated Security Risk Assessment of Critical Supply Chains

The Cooperation Group, in cooperation with the Commission and ENISA, may carry out coordinated security risk assessments of specific critical ICT supply chains, considering technical and non-technical risk factors. The Commission designates which services, systems, or products may be subject to such assessment after consulting the Cooperation Group and ENISA.

Art. 23

Incident Reporting

Essential and important entities must notify their CSIRT or competent authority of any significant incident without undue delay. Notifications follow a staged approach:

  1. Early warning — within 24 hours of becoming aware, indicating whether the incident is suspected to be caused by unlawful or malicious acts and whether it could have cross-border impact.
  2. Incident notification — within 72 hours of becoming aware, updating the early warning with an initial assessment of the incident's severity and impact, and where available, the indicators of compromise.
  3. Intermediate report — on request of the CSIRT or competent authority.
  4. Final report — within one month of the incident notification, containing: a detailed description including severity and impact; the type of threat or root cause that likely triggered the incident; mitigation measures applied and ongoing; and where applicable, the cross-border impact. Where the incident is ongoing at the one-month mark, the entity submits a progress report at that time and a final report within one month of completing handling.

The CSIRT or competent authority must provide initial feedback within 24 hours of receiving the early warning, and on request, guidance or operational advice on mitigation. Where public awareness is necessary to prevent or deal with the incident, or disclosure is otherwise in the public interest, the entity's CSIRT or competent authority (or the entity itself) may inform the public.

Trust service providers must notify within 24 hours. DNS service providers, TLD registries, cloud computing, data centre, content delivery network, managed service providers, managed security service providers, and online marketplaces, search engines, and social networking platforms must comply with implementing acts on reporting.

Art. 24–25

European Cybersecurity Certification and Standardisation

Member states may require essential and important entities to use particular ICT products, services, or processes that are certified under European cybersecurity certification schemes. Member states must also encourage entities to use qualified trust services. The Commission may adopt delegated acts specifying which categories of entities must use certified ICT products, services, or processes.

To promote consistent implementation, member states must encourage the use of European and international standards and technical specifications relevant to the security of network and information systems, without mandating or discriminating in favour of a particular technology.

Chapter VJurisdiction and Registration
Art. 26

Jurisdiction

Entities are considered under the jurisdiction of the member state in which they are established. For certain cross-border digital providers (DNS service providers, TLD registries, cloud computing, data centres, CDNs, managed service and security service providers, online marketplaces, search engines, and social networking platforms), jurisdiction lies with the member state where the entity has its main establishment in the EU — meaning the member state where cybersecurity risk-management decisions are predominantly taken, or the member state where the entity has the most employees. If the entity is not established in the EU but offers services within it, it must designate a representative in one of the member states where its services are offered.

Art. 27–28

Registration

DNS service providers, TLD registries, entities providing domain registration services, cloud computing, data centre, CDN, managed service, managed security service providers, and online marketplaces, search engines, and social networking platforms must submit to their competent authority: name, address, contact details, relevant sector and subsector, and a list of member states where they provide services. They must notify any changes within three months. ENISA creates and maintains a registry of these entities based on member state submissions.

TLD registries and entities providing domain registration services must collect and maintain accurate and complete domain name registration data (WHOIS data), including: the domain name, the registration date, the registrant's name, email, and phone number, and the contact email and phone for the administrative and technical point of contact. They must establish policies and procedures for ensuring data accuracy, make non-personal registration data publicly available without undue delay, and provide access to specific personal registration data to legitimate access seekers upon a duly justified request — within 72 hours. Member states must maintain a list of legitimate access seekers. This is without prejudice to GDPR.

Chapter VIInformation Sharing
Art. 29–30

Cybersecurity Information-Sharing Arrangements and Voluntary Notification

Member states must ensure that essential and important entities, and where appropriate their suppliers and service providers, are able to exchange relevant cybersecurity information among themselves, including cyber threats, near misses, vulnerabilities, tools, tactics, techniques, indicators of compromise, adversarial tactics, threat-actor information, and cybersecurity alerts. Sharing must respect trade secrets and GDPR. Member states must facilitate the establishment of cybersecurity information-sharing arrangements, which must specify operational elements (e.g., designated platforms, automation, rules of engagement) and may involve public authorities.

Entities may voluntarily notify their national CSIRT or competent authority of near misses, cyber threats, and incidents that do not rise to the threshold of "significant" — without incurring additional obligations from the notification.

Chapter VIISupervision and Enforcement
Art. 31

General Aspects of Supervision and Enforcement

Member states must ensure that competent authorities effectively monitor compliance and take necessary measures. Member states may allow competent authorities to prioritise supervisory tasks using a risk-based approach. Competent authorities must cooperate closely with personal data protection authorities when addressing incidents involving personal data breaches.

Art. 32

Supervision for Essential Entities

Competent authorities may subject essential entities to: on-site and off-site inspections, including random checks by trained professionals; regular and targeted security audits carried out by an independent body or the authority itself; ad hoc audits triggered by a significant incident or infringement; security scans based on objective, non-discriminatory, fair, and transparent risk-assessment criteria; requests for information necessary for post-incident assessment of cybersecurity risk-management measures; and requests for access to data, documents, and information needed for supervision.

Where these measures reveal non-compliance, authorities can: issue binding instructions requiring specific risk-management measures or incident-reporting compliance; order the entity to implement security audit recommendations within a set time; order the entity to bring measures into compliance in a specified manner and time; impose administrative fines; and request that relevant national courts or authorities issue a temporary ban on any natural person with management responsibilities (CEO, legal representative) from exercising managerial functions in that entity. Temporary bans can only be issued as a last resort and last only until the entity takes necessary remedial action.

Art. 33

Supervision for Important Entities

Supervision for important entities is ex post — competent authorities take action when provided with evidence, indication, or information suggesting non-compliance. Authorities may carry out: on-site and off-site inspections; targeted security audits by an independent body or the authority; security scans; and requests for information and access to data. Enforcement powers are the same as for essential entities, except that the temporary management ban is not available for important entities.

Art. 34

General Conditions for Administrative Fines

Administrative fines must be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive, and assessed on a case-by-case basis. When setting the amount, account must be taken of: nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement; whether it was intentional or negligent; actions taken to mitigate damage; degree of responsibility; previous infringements; degree of cooperation with the authority; affected categories of personal data; and any other aggravating or mitigating factor.

For essential entities: up to €10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

For important entities: up to €7 million or 1.4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Member states decide whether and to what extent administrative fines can be imposed on public administration entities. Where a member state's legal system does not provide for administrative fines, it must ensure that effective, proportionate, and dissuasive penalties are applied through other means — the Commission must be notified of these arrangements.

Art. 35–36

Infringements Involving Personal Data Breaches and Penalties

Where an incident involves a personal data breach under the GDPR, any administrative fine imposed under this Directive for the same conduct must not duplicate the GDPR fine. Data protection authorities retain their full powers. Where the competent authority under this Directive is not the data protection authority, they must cooperate and exchange information. If an entity is supervised in another member state for GDPR purposes, the competent authority under this Directive must notify the relevant data protection authority.

Member states must lay down rules on penalties (including administrative fines) for infringements and ensure they are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. They must notify the Commission of those rules by 17 October 2024 and of any subsequent amendment.

Chapter VIIIDelegated and Implementing Acts
Art. 37–39

Delegated and Implementing Powers

The Commission has power to adopt delegated acts to supplement this Directive by specifying which categories of essential and important entities must use certified ICT products, services, or processes. The delegation is granted for five years (renewable), revocable by Parliament or Council.

The Commission may adopt implementing acts specifying: technical and methodological requirements for the cybersecurity risk-management measures in Article 21 for specific entity types; and the cases, format, and timelines for incident notifications. These implementing acts follow the committee examination procedure.

By 17 October 2024 (and periodically thereafter), the Commission must adopt implementing acts for certain cross-border digital service providers (DNS, TLD, cloud, data centre, CDN, managed service/security, online marketplaces, search engines, social networking) specifying when an incident is considered significant and the detailed format and procedures for notifications.

Chapter IXFinal Provisions
Art. 40

Review

The Commission must review this Directive by 17 October 2027 and every 36 months thereafter, reporting to the European Parliament and Council.

Art. 41

Transposition

Member states must adopt and publish the national laws necessary to comply with this Directive by 17 October 2024 and apply them from 18 October 2024. Where sector-specific legislation equivalent to this Directive already exists, member states must notify the Commission.

Art. 44

Repeal

Directive (EU) 2016/1148 (the original NIS Directive) was repealed on 18 October 2024. References to the old Directive are read as references to this Directive.

Art. 46

Entry into Force

This Directive entered into force on 16 January 2023. It is addressed to the member states.

ReferenceAnnexes I–II — Covered Sectors
Summary

What each Annex covers

Annex I — Sectors of High Criticality (Essential) — Energy — electricity (undertakings, distribution system operators, transmission system operators, producers, nominated electricity market operators, market participants providing aggregation, demand response, or energy storage, operators of electric vehicle charging points), district heating and cooling, oil (operators of oil pipelines, oil production, refining, treatment, storage, and transmission facilities, central stockholding entities), gas (supply undertakings, distribution system operators, transmission system operators, storage system operators, LNG system operators, natural gas undertakings, operators of natural gas refining and treatment facilities), hydrogen (operators of hydrogen production, storage, and transmission). Transport — air (air carriers, airport managing bodies, traffic management control operators), rail (infrastructure managers, railway undertakings, service facility operators), water (inland waterway, maritime and coastal transport companies, port managing bodies, vessel traffic services), road (road authorities responsible for traffic management control, operators of intelligent transport systems). Banking — credit institutions. Financial market infrastructures — operators of trading venues, central counterparties. Health — healthcare providers, EU reference laboratories, entities carrying out R&D on medicinal products, entities manufacturing basic pharmaceutical products and preparations, entities manufacturing critical medical devices (during a public health emergency). Drinking water — suppliers and distributors of water intended for human consumption (excluding those for whom water distribution is a non-essential part of their general activity). Waste water — undertakings collecting, disposing of, or treating urban or domestic or industrial waste water. Digital infrastructure — internet exchange point providers, DNS service providers, TLD name registries, cloud computing service providers, data centre service providers, content delivery network providers, trust service providers, providers of public electronic communications networks, and providers of publicly available electronic communications services. ICT service management (B2B) — managed service providers and managed security service providers. Public administration — central government entities (excluding judiciary, parliaments, and central banks). Space — operators of ground-based infrastructure supporting space-based services.

Annex II — Other Critical Sectors (Important) — Postal and courier services. Waste management — undertakings carrying out waste management (excluding those for whom waste management is not their main economic activity). Manufacture, production, and distribution of chemicals. Food production, processing, and distribution — food businesses engaged in wholesale distribution, industrial production, or processing. Manufacturing — of medical devices and in vitro diagnostic medical devices; computer, electronic, and optical products; electrical equipment; machinery and equipment n.e.c.; motor vehicles, trailers, and semi-trailers; other transport equipment. Digital providers — providers of online marketplaces, online search engines, and social networking services platforms. Research — research organisations.

This reference covers what the Directive says. This is a Directive, not a Regulation — it binds member states to achieve a result, but each country transposes it into national law, which may contain stricter or more detailed requirements. Working out whether your entity is essential or important, which member state has jurisdiction, and what your national transposition adds — is a different exercise.
