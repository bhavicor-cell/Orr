Chapter IPreliminary
Sec. 1

Short Title and Commencement

This is the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. It does not come into force all at once — the Central Government appoints different dates for different provisions by notification in the Official Gazette.

Sec. 2

Definitions — selected key terms

Board — the Data Protection Board of India, set up under Section 18 of this Act.

Child — anyone who has not turned eighteen.

Consent Manager — a registered intermediary that gives a Data Principal a single place to give, manage, review, and withdraw consent, through an accessible, transparent, and interoperable platform.

Data Fiduciary — whoever decides why and how personal data gets processed. Can be a person, a company, or a government body. If multiple parties decide together, each one is a Data Fiduciary.

Data Principal — the person the data is about. If that person is a child, the term includes her parents or lawful guardian. If that person has a disability and a lawful guardian, it includes the guardian acting on her behalf.

Data Processor — whoever processes personal data on behalf of a Data Fiduciary. Think: the cloud provider, the analytics vendor, the payroll outsourcer.

Data Protection Officer — a person appointed by a Significant Data Fiduciary under Section 10. Not every Data Fiduciary needs one — only those classified as Significant.

Digital personal data — personal data in digital form.

Personal data — any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to that data.

Personal data breach — any unauthorised processing, or any accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction, or loss of access to personal data, that compromises its confidentiality, integrity, or availability.

Processing — any automated or partly automated operation on digital personal data. The list is broad: collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation, retrieval, use, alignment, combination, indexing, sharing, disclosure, dissemination, restriction, erasure, destruction.

Significant Data Fiduciary — a Data Fiduciary or class of Data Fiduciaries that the Central Government has specifically notified under Section 10. Carries heavier obligations.

Specified purpose — the purpose stated in the notice the Data Fiduciary gives the Data Principal. This is what the consent is tied to.

Person — defined broadly: includes individuals, Hindu undivided families, companies, firms, associations of persons, the State, and any artificial juristic person.

Appellate Tribunal — the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT). Yes, the telecom tribunal — this Act routes appeals through it.

Sec. 3

Application

This Act applies to the processing of digital personal data within India — whether the data was collected digitally or collected in non-digital form and digitised later.

It also applies to processing outside India, if that processing is connected to offering goods or services to people in India. Being based abroad does not exempt you if your product serves Indian users.

Two carve-outs: personal data processed by an individual for purely personal or domestic purposes is outside scope, and personal data that the Data Principal herself made publicly available — or that someone was legally obliged to make public — is outside scope.

Chapter IIObligations of Data Fiduciary
Sec. 4

Grounds for Processing

You can process someone's personal data only for a lawful purpose — meaning any purpose not expressly forbidden by law — and only on one of two grounds: the Data Principal gave consent, or the processing falls under the "certain legitimate uses" listed in Section 7.

Sec. 5

Notice

Before asking for consent, the Data Fiduciary must give the Data Principal a notice. The notice must tell her: what personal data is being collected and why; how she can exercise her rights (withdrawing consent, accessing data, correcting or erasing it); and how she can complain to the Board.

The notice must be available in English or any language in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution.

If consent was already given before this Act came into force, the Data Fiduciary must still send this notice as soon as reasonably practicable. Processing can continue in the meantime, unless the Data Principal withdraws consent.

Sec. 6

Consent

Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous. It requires a clear affirmative action — pre-ticked boxes will not do. Consent is limited to the personal data that is actually necessary for the stated purpose.

Any part of consent that violates this Act, its rules, or any other law is automatically invalid to that extent. You cannot bundle a waiver of the right to complain into a consent form.

Consent requests must be in clear, plain language, with access in English or any Eighth Schedule language, and must include contact details for the Data Protection Officer or another authorised person.

Withdrawal is a right. The Data Principal can withdraw consent at any time, and doing so must be as easy as giving it was. The consequences of withdrawal fall on the Data Principal — the platform may stop serving her — but withdrawal does not retroactively make earlier processing unlawful.

Once consent is withdrawn, the Data Fiduciary must stop processing within a reasonable time and must cause its Data Processors to stop too — unless processing without consent is separately authorised under this Act or any other Indian law.

A Data Principal can manage consent through a Consent Manager instead of dealing with each Data Fiduciary directly. The Consent Manager acts on her behalf, is accountable to her, and must be registered with the Board under prescribed conditions.

If a question arises in proceedings about whether consent existed, the Data Fiduciary bears the burden of proof. It must prove that notice was given and valid consent was obtained.

Sec. 7

Certain Legitimate Uses

These are the situations where personal data can be processed without the Data Principal's consent:

(a) The Data Principal voluntarily gave her data for a specified purpose and has not told the Data Fiduciary she objects to its use.

(b) The State or its instrumentalities need it to provide a subsidy, benefit, service, certificate, licence, or permit — either because the Data Principal previously consented to government processing, or because the data already sits in a government database the Central Government has notified. Processing must follow government policy or applicable law.

(c) The State needs it to perform any function under Indian law, or to protect sovereignty, integrity, or security.

(d) Someone needs it to meet a legal obligation to disclose information to the State.

(e) Compliance with any judgment, decree, or order under Indian law — or any judgment or order on contractual or civil claims under foreign law.

(f) A medical emergency threatening life or immediate health.

(g) Medical treatment or health services during an epidemic, disease outbreak, or public health threat.

(h) Safety or assistance during a disaster or breakdown of public order.

(i) Employment purposes, or protecting the employer from loss or liability — preventing corporate espionage, maintaining confidentiality of trade secrets, intellectual property, or classified information, or providing a service or benefit the employee has sought.

Sec. 8

General Obligations of Data Fiduciary

The Data Fiduciary is on the hook for compliance with this entire Act — regardless of any contract term saying otherwise, and regardless of whether the Data Principal failed to do her own duties. This responsibility extends to anything a Data Processor does on the Data Fiduciary's behalf.

A Data Processor can only be engaged under a valid contract.

If personal data is going to be used to make a decision that affects the Data Principal, or shared with another Data Fiduciary, the data must be complete, accurate, and consistent.

Every Data Fiduciary must put in place appropriate technical and organisational measures, and must take reasonable security safeguards to prevent breaches.

When a breach happens, the Data Fiduciary must notify both the Board and every affected Data Principal, in the prescribed form and manner.

Personal data must be erased when the Data Principal withdraws consent or when it is reasonable to assume the specified purpose is no longer being served — whichever comes first. The only exception is where retention is required by another law. The Data Fiduciary must also make its Data Processors erase data that was shared with them.

The specified purpose is treated as "no longer being served" if the Data Principal has not contacted the Data Fiduciary about that purpose and has not exercised any of her data rights for a prescribed period. Different periods can be prescribed for different types of Data Fiduciaries and different purposes.

Every Data Fiduciary must publish the business contact details of its Data Protection Officer (or a person who can answer questions about processing) and must set up an effective grievance redressal mechanism.

Sec. 9

Children's Data

Before processing any personal data of a child, or of a person with disability who has a lawful guardian, the Data Fiduciary must get verifiable consent from the parent or guardian.

No Data Fiduciary may process a child's data in a way that is likely to harm the child's well-being.

Tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising directed at children are banned.

The Central Government can exempt specific classes of Data Fiduciaries or specific purposes from the verifiable-consent and tracking restrictions, subject to conditions. It can also notify an age above which a Data Fiduciary that processes children's data in a verifiably safe manner is exempt from some or all of these obligations.

Sec. 10

Significant Data Fiduciary

The Central Government can designate any Data Fiduciary or class as a Significant Data Fiduciary, based on: how much personal data they process and how sensitive it is; risk to Data Principals' rights; potential impact on sovereignty, integrity, or security; risk to electoral democracy; and public order.

Once designated, a Significant Data Fiduciary must: appoint a Data Protection Officer who is based in India, reports to the board of directors, represents the entity under this Act, and acts as the grievance contact point; appoint an independent data auditor; and carry out periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments, periodic audits, and any other prescribed measures.

Chapter IIIRights and Duties of Data Principal
Sec. 11

Right to Access Information

A Data Principal can ask any Data Fiduciary she has given consent to for: a summary of her personal data being processed and what processing activities are being done; the identities of every Data Fiduciary and Data Processor the data has been shared with, and what was shared; and any other prescribed information about her data and its processing.

The sharing-identity and additional-information rights do not apply where data was shared with a Data Fiduciary authorised by law to obtain it for preventing, detecting, investigating, or prosecuting offences or cyber incidents.

Sec. 12

Right to Correction and Erasure

A Data Principal can ask for her personal data to be corrected if it is inaccurate or misleading, completed if incomplete, updated if outdated, and erased. On an erasure request, the Data Fiduciary must erase unless retention is necessary for the specified purpose or required by law.

Sec. 13

Right of Grievance Redressal

Every Data Fiduciary and Consent Manager must provide a readily available grievance mechanism. They must respond within the prescribed period. The Data Principal must exhaust this mechanism before going to the Board.

Sec. 14

Right to Nominate

A Data Principal can nominate someone to exercise her data rights in the event of her death or incapacity. Incapacity means inability to exercise rights due to unsoundness of mind or infirmity of body.

Sec. 15

Duties of Data Principal

Data Principals have duties too. They must: comply with all applicable laws while exercising their rights; not impersonate anyone while providing personal data; not suppress material information when providing data for government documents, identifiers, or proofs; not file false or frivolous grievances or complaints; and only furnish verifiably authentic information when requesting corrections or erasure.

Chapter IVSpecial Provisions
Sec. 16

Cross-Border Transfer

The Central Government can restrict the transfer of personal data for processing to any country or territory it specifies by notification. Any other Indian law that imposes stricter restrictions on cross-border transfer continues to apply on top.

Sec. 17

Exemptions

Most of this Act's Data Fiduciary obligations (Chapter II, except the security and breach obligations) and all of the Data Principal's rights (Chapter III) and the cross-border rules (Section 16) do not apply when processing is needed for: enforcing a legal right or claim; judicial, quasi-judicial, regulatory, or supervisory functions of courts, tribunals, or statutory bodies; prevention, detection, investigation, or prosecution of offences; processing data of non-Indian Data Principals under a contract with a foreign entity; a court-approved merger, amalgamation, demerger, or company restructuring; or ascertaining financial information of a loan defaulter.

The entire Act does not apply to: processing by State instrumentalities notified by the Central Government in the interest of sovereignty, integrity, security, foreign relations, public order, or preventing incitement to cognisable offences — including the Central Government's own processing of data furnished by those instrumentalities. Processing for research, archiving, or statistical purposes is also exempt, provided the data is not used for decisions specific to a Data Principal and the processing follows prescribed standards.

The Central Government can exempt specific Data Fiduciaries or classes, including startups, from the notice requirement, data-accuracy and erasure obligations, and the Significant Data Fiduciary and access-information rules.

When the State processes personal data: the erasure obligation and right to erasure do not apply. Where the State's processing does not include making decisions affecting the Data Principal, the correction and updating rights also do not apply.

The Central Government has a five-year window from commencement to declare that any provision of this Act does not apply to specified Data Fiduciaries or classes, for a specified period.

Chapter VData Protection Board of India
Sec. 18–19

Establishment and Composition

The Central Government establishes the Data Protection Board of India. It is a body corporate — perpetual succession, common seal, can hold property, enter contracts, and sue or be sued. Its headquarters are where the Central Government notifies.

The Board has a Chairperson and as many Members as the Central Government decides. Members must have ability, integrity, and standing, with special knowledge or practical experience in: data governance, administration, consumer or social protection, dispute resolution, information and communication technology, digital economy, law, regulation, or techno-regulation. At least one Member must be a law expert.

Sec. 20–22

Terms, Disqualifications, Vacancies

Chairperson and Members serve two-year terms, eligible for re-appointment. Salary and conditions cannot be changed to their disadvantage after appointment.

Disqualifications: insolvency; conviction involving moral turpitude; physical or mental incapacity; conflicting financial interest; or abuse of position. Removal requires a hearing.

Resignation takes effect from the earliest of: date the Central Government permits, three months after notice, successor's appointment, or term expiry. One-year cooling-off on employment after leaving, with Central Government approval required. Any subsequent employment with a Data Fiduciary the member had proceedings against must be disclosed.

Sec. 23–26

Proceedings, Staff, Powers of Chairperson

The Board follows prescribed meeting and authentication procedures. Defects in constitution or procedure do not invalidate proceedings unless they affect the merits. The Board can hire staff with Central Government approval. All Board personnel are deemed public servants. The Chairperson has general superintendence over administration, can authorise scrutiny of complaints, and allocates proceedings among Members.

Chapter VIPowers, Functions and Procedure of Board
Sec. 27

Powers and Functions

The Board can inquire into and penalise: personal data breaches reported under Section 8(6); complaints from Data Principals about a Data Fiduciary's obligations or about their rights being denied; complaints about Consent Manager failures; breaches of Consent Manager registration conditions; and Central Government referrals about intermediary non-compliance under Section 37(2). On a breach report, the Board can direct urgent remedial or mitigation measures.

The Board can issue binding directions to anyone after a hearing. It can later modify, suspend, withdraw, or cancel those directions on application or government reference.

Sec. 28

Procedure

The Board is independent and operates as a digital office by design — complaints, allocation, hearings, and decisions happen digitally. On receiving a matter, the Board decides whether grounds exist to proceed. If not, it closes the case with written reasons. If yes, it conducts inquiry under the principles of natural justice, recording reasons throughout.

The Board has civil-court powers: summoning and examining witnesses on oath, requiring document production, inspecting records. But it cannot block access to premises or seize equipment in a way that disrupts day-to-day operations. It can requisition police or government officers. It can issue interim orders mid-inquiry after hearing the affected person. On completion, it either closes the case or proceeds to penalties. False or frivolous complaints can earn a warning or costs.

Chapter VIIAppeal and Alternate Dispute Resolution
Sec. 29

Appeal to Appellate Tribunal

Anyone aggrieved by a Board order can appeal to the Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) within sixty days, in the prescribed form with the prescribed fee. Late appeals are allowed if sufficient cause is shown. The Tribunal can confirm, modify, or set aside the order after hearing both sides. Target disposal: six months. If missed, reasons must be recorded. The Tribunal also operates as a digital office. Further appeals from the Tribunal follow Section 18 of the TRAI Act.

Sec. 30

Execution

Tribunal orders are executable as civil court decrees. The Tribunal can also send orders to a local civil court for execution.

Sec. 31

Mediation

If the Board thinks a complaint can be resolved through mediation, it can direct the parties to try, using a mutually agreed mediator or one provided under Indian law.

Sec. 32

Voluntary Undertaking

At any stage of proceedings, the Board can accept a voluntary undertaking from a person — a commitment to take or refrain from certain actions within a set time. Accepting the undertaking bars further proceedings on those matters. But if the person breaks the undertaking, the breach is treated as a breach of this Act, and the Board can proceed to penalties.

Chapter VIIIPenalties and Adjudication
Sec. 33

Penalties

If, after completing its inquiry, the Board finds a significant breach, it can impose the monetary penalties set out in the Schedule — after giving the person a hearing.

When setting the amount, the Board weighs: how serious the breach was and how long it lasted; what kind of personal data was affected; whether it was a repeat breach; whether the person gained or avoided loss from it; what they did to mitigate and how quickly; whether the penalty is proportionate and effective as a deterrent; and the likely impact on the person.

Sec. 34

Where the Money Goes

All penalty amounts go to the Consolidated Fund of India.

Chapter IXMiscellaneous
Sec. 35

Good-Faith Protection

No suit, prosecution, or legal proceedings can be brought against the Central Government, the Board, its Chairperson, or any Member, officer, or employee for anything done in good faith under this Act.

Sec. 36

Power to Call for Information

The Central Government can require the Board, any Data Fiduciary, or any intermediary to hand over whatever information it asks for.

Sec. 37

Blocking Access

If the Board has penalised a Data Fiduciary two or more times and recommends, in the public interest, that its digital platform be blocked — the Central Government or its authorised officer can, after hearing the Data Fiduciary and recording written reasons, direct any agency or intermediary to block public access. Every intermediary that receives such a direction must comply.

Sec. 38

Relationship with Other Laws

This Act sits on top of every other law — it does not replace them. But where this Act conflicts with another law, this Act wins to the extent of the conflict.

Sec. 39

Bar of Jurisdiction

No civil court can hear any matter that falls within the Board's powers. No court or authority can grant an injunction against action taken under this Act.

Sec. 40

Power to Make Rules

The Central Government makes the rules that fill in the detail throughout this Act — how notices work, how Consent Managers register, how breaches are reported, what the erasure time periods are, how grievance mechanisms operate, what the Data Protection Impact Assessment covers, how the Board functions, and how appeals are filed. Rules must be published in draft before finalisation.

Sec. 41

Parliamentary Oversight

Every rule and every notification under Sections 16 and 42 must be laid before Parliament for thirty days. If both Houses agree to modify or annul it, the change takes effect — but does not undo anything already done under the original rule.

Sec. 42

Amending the Penalty Schedule

The Central Government can change the penalty amounts by notification, but no penalty can be raised beyond twice the amount originally specified when the Act was first enacted.

Sec. 43

Removing Difficulties

If something in this Act creates a practical difficulty, the Central Government can issue orders to fix it — but only within three years of commencement, and each order must be laid before Parliament.

Sec. 44

Changes to Other Acts

TDSAT's jurisdiction is expanded to cover appeals under this Act. In the IT Act, 2000: Section 43A (compensation for failure to protect data under reasonable security practices) is deleted; the proviso to Section 81 is expanded to include this Act; and Section 87(2)(ob) (rules on sensitive personal data practices) is deleted. In the RTI Act, 2005: the personal-information exemption in Section 8(1)(j) is simplified to cover "information which relates to personal information."

Penalty Table

  1. Failing to take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach (Section 8(5)) — up to ₹250 crore.
  2. Failing to notify the Board or affected Data Principals of a breach (Section 8(6)) — up to ₹200 crore.
  3. Breaching obligations regarding children's personal data (Section 9) — up to ₹200 crore.
  4. Breaching additional Significant Data Fiduciary obligations (Section 10) — up to ₹150 crore.
  5. Data Principal breaching her duties (Section 15) — up to ₹10,000.
  6. Breaching any term of a voluntary undertaking accepted by the Board (Section 32) — up to the penalty that would have applied to the original breach.
  7. Any other breach of this Act or its rules — up to ₹50 crore.
