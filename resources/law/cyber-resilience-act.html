Chapter IGeneral Provisions
Art. 1

Subject Matter

This Regulation sets cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements that are placed on the EU market. It covers: the design, development, and production of these products; vulnerability handling by manufacturers; and the conditions for making these products available commercially. It applies to hardware and software — including remote data processing solutions where the data processing is essential for the product to function.

Art. 2

Scope

This Regulation applies to any product with digital elements that is made available on the EU market and whose intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection to a device or network.

It does not apply to: products already covered by specific EU rules with equivalent cybersecurity requirements (medical devices, in vitro diagnostic devices, civil aviation, motor vehicles, products certified under the EU Marine Equipment Directive); open-source software that is not supplied in the course of a commercial activity; cloud services and SaaS that are not remote data processing solutions for a product with digital elements; or products exclusively developed for national security, military, or defence purposes, or specifically designed to process classified information.

Where a product falls under both this Regulation and other EU legislation, both apply — but where the other legislation's cybersecurity requirements are at least equivalent, those specific requirements of the other legislation are deemed to satisfy this Regulation's corresponding requirements.

Art. 3

Definitions — selected key terms

Product with digital elements — any software or hardware product, and its remote data processing solutions, including software or hardware components placed on the market separately. The defining feature is a data connection to a device or network.

Remote data processing solution — data processing at a distance for which the software is designed and developed by or on behalf of the manufacturer, and the absence of which would prevent the product from performing one of its functions.

Manufacturer — whoever develops or manufactures a product with digital elements (or has it developed or manufactured) and markets it under their own name or trademark, whether for payment, monetisation, or free of charge.

Software — the part of an electronic information system that consists of computer code.

Hardware — a physical electronic information system, or part of one, that can process, store, or transmit digital data.

Vulnerability — a weakness, susceptibility, or flaw of a product that can be exploited by a cyber threat.

Actively exploited vulnerability — a vulnerability for which there is reliable evidence that a malicious actor has exploited it in a system without the system owner's permission.

Substantial modification — a change to the product after it has been placed on the market that affects compliance with the essential requirements, or changes the intended purpose for which the product was assessed. Software updates that merely maintain the product's intended security level are not substantial modifications.

Important product — a product with digital elements whose core function falls into a category listed in Annex III, meaning a vulnerability in it could lead to serious downstream cybersecurity effects. Two classes: Class I (identity management, browsers, password managers, VPNs, network management, security information and event management tools, routers, modems, microcontrollers, industrial automation, IoT devices, and others) and Class II (higher risk: hypervisors, firewalls, intrusion detection/prevention, microprocessors with security features, operating systems, industrial routers and switches, public key infrastructure, secure elements, hardware security modules, smartcards, smart meters, and others).

Critical product — a product with digital elements whose core function falls into a category listed in Annex IV, meaning a vulnerability in it poses a severe risk due to its central role in system security. Currently: hardware devices with security boxes, smart meter gateways, smartcards and similar devices, and secure elements.

Open-source software steward — a legal person (other than a manufacturer) that systematically supports the development of free and open-source software products intended for commercial activities and plays a main role in ensuring the viability of those products.

Economic operator — the manufacturer, authorised representative, importer, or distributor of a product with digital elements, or the open-source software steward.

Chapter IIObligations of Economic Operators
Art. 13

Obligations of Manufacturers

Manufacturers bear the heaviest obligations. When placing a product on the market, they must ensure it was designed, developed, and produced in accordance with the essential cybersecurity requirements in Annex I. This includes carrying out a cybersecurity risk assessment, taking the results into account during planning and design, and documenting the assessment in the technical documentation.

Manufacturers must exercise due diligence when integrating third-party components, ensuring those components do not compromise the product's cybersecurity — including when using open-source software components. They must document the software bill of materials covering at minimum the top-level dependencies.

Once a product is on the market, the manufacturer must handle vulnerabilities effectively for the entire support period (at least five years from market entry, unless the product's expected lifetime is shorter). This means: identifying and documenting vulnerabilities including through third-party component tracking; applying security fixes without delay; providing free security updates; disclosing fixed vulnerabilities publicly including in the EUVD; and establishing a coordinated vulnerability disclosure policy with a contact address.

Manufacturers must draw up technical documentation before market entry, carry out the applicable conformity assessment procedure, issue an EU declaration of conformity, and affix the CE marking. They must keep documentation for ten years. If a product is not in conformity, the manufacturer must immediately take corrective action, withdraw, or recall. They must cooperate with market surveillance authorities on request.

The manufacturer must include the product's name, version, purpose, contact information, and a link to its support and vulnerability-handling processes on the product or packaging or documentation.

Art. 14

Reporting Obligations

Manufacturers must report to the designated CSIRT and ENISA: any actively exploited vulnerability contained in the product — within 24 hours (early warning), 72 hours (vulnerability notification), and 14 days (final report); and any severe incident that impacts the product's security — within the same timeframes. Early warnings must include: the product, general nature of the exploit, and whether other member states may be affected. Final reports must include: a description of the vulnerability, its severity and impact, the fix status, and any available corrective measures. ENISA forwards non-identifying information to the single point of contact of each affected member state and to the designated CSIRT. National CSIRTs may request the full report.

Manufacturers must also inform affected users of the vulnerability and, where possible, of mitigation actions, in a timely manner and in a language easily understood by users.

Art. 15

Voluntary Reporting

Manufacturers and other natural or legal persons may voluntarily report any vulnerability or cyber threat to the designated CSIRT or ENISA.

Art. 16

Establishment of a Single Reporting Platform

ENISA must develop and maintain a single electronic reporting platform for manufacturers to submit notifications. The platform connects to the member state CSIRTs and includes role-based access controls for authorities.

Art. 17

Other Reporting

Where a manufacturer reports a serious incident under the NIS2 Directive, the reporting obligation under this Regulation is deemed satisfied to the extent the NIS2 report covers the same information with equivalent timing.

Art. 18

Authorised Representatives

A manufacturer may appoint an EU-based authorised representative by written mandate. The representative must at minimum: verify that the conformity assessment, technical documentation, and CE marking procedures have been carried out; keep documentation available for ten years; cooperate with authorities; and terminate the mandate if the manufacturer acts contrary to this Regulation.

Art. 19–20

Importers and Distributors

Importers may place a product on the EU market only if the manufacturer has completed the conformity assessment, drawn up technical documentation, affixed the CE marking, and included required contact and identification information. If an importer considers a product is not in conformity, it must not place the product on the market and must inform the manufacturer and market surveillance authority. Importers must keep a copy of the EU declaration of conformity for ten years.

Distributors must verify, before making a product available, that it bears the CE marking, includes required documentation and information, and that the manufacturer and importer have met their obligations. If a distributor considers a product is not in conformity, it must not make it available until compliance is established, and must inform the manufacturer, importer, and market surveillance authority. Storage and transport must not jeopardise compliance.

Art. 21

Responsibility Along the Supply Chain

An importer or distributor becomes a manufacturer — and inherits all manufacturer obligations — if it places the product on the market under its own name or trademark, or makes a substantial modification. The original manufacturer must cooperate and provide access to necessary information.

Art. 22

Other Economic Operators

Any natural or legal person (other than a manufacturer, importer, or distributor) that carries out a substantial modification to the product is treated as a manufacturer for that product and must comply with all manufacturer obligations from the point of modification.

Art. 24

Open-Source Software Stewards

Open-source software stewards must: establish and document a cybersecurity policy to foster development of a secure product (including coordinated vulnerability disclosure); cooperate with market surveillance authorities; report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents through the same channels and timelines as manufacturers (Art. 14); and provide documentation required by Annex II in a way that facilitates the identification and documentation of vulnerabilities. Stewards are subject to a lighter obligation set than manufacturers — they are not required to complete conformity assessment, affix CE marking, or issue a declaration of conformity.

Chapter IIIConformity of the Product
Art. 27–28

Presumption of Conformity

Products that comply with harmonised European standards (or parts of them) published in the Official Journal are presumed to conform to the essential requirements those standards cover. If no harmonised standard exists or covers the requirement, the Commission can adopt common specifications by implementing act. Compliance with common specifications also triggers the presumption of conformity.

Products certified under an EU cybersecurity certification scheme at assurance level "substantial" or "high" are presumed to conform to the essential requirements the certificate covers, as long as the certificate or the scheme covers them.

Art. 29–30

EU Declaration of Conformity and CE Marking

The EU declaration of conformity states that the essential requirements have been demonstrated to be met. It is issued per the template in Annex V, must be kept up to date, and translated into the required languages. By drawing up the declaration, the manufacturer takes responsibility for compliance. The CE marking is affixed visibly, legibly, and indelibly before market entry. Where other EU legislation also requires CE marking, the marking indicates conformity with all applicable legislation.

Art. 32

Conformity Assessment Procedures

Default products — products not listed in Annex III or IV — can be self-assessed by the manufacturer through internal control (Annex VIII, Part I). Important products (Annex III, Class I) must use: a harmonised standard or common specification covering all essential requirements (in which case internal control suffices), or EU-type examination plus internal production control, or conformity assessment based on full quality assurance. Important products (Class II) and critical products (Annex IV) require third-party assessment: EU-type examination plus internal production control, or full quality assurance. The Commission can require a European cybersecurity certificate at assurance level "substantial" or "high" for critical products.

Where a manufacturer has not applied a harmonised standard or where no harmonised standard exists, important and critical products require third-party conformity assessment.

Art. 31

Technical Documentation

Manufacturers must draw up technical documentation before market entry, keep it up to date for the support period (or ten years, whichever is longer), and make it available to market surveillance authorities on request. The content requirements are set out in Annex VII.

Art. 33–34

Support Period

The support period is determined by the manufacturer based on expected product lifetime, user expectations, market benchmarks, and Commission guidance — but must be at least five years, except where the product's expected use life is shorter than five years. During this period, the manufacturer must provide security updates and handle vulnerabilities. The support period must be clearly indicated before purchase. The Commission can require minimum support periods by delegated act.

Chapter IVNotification of Conformity Assessment Bodies
Art. 35–51

Notified Bodies

Each member state designates a notifying authority responsible for setting up and carrying out procedures for vetting conformity assessment bodies ("notified bodies"). Notified bodies must be independent, impartial, competent, technically qualified, free of conflicts of interest, and have adequate cybersecurity expertise and resources. They carry liability insurance and maintain professional secrecy.

Notified bodies must participate in standardisation activities and the relevant European coordination group. They inform the notifying authority and other notified bodies of refused, restricted, suspended, or withdrawn certificates. The Commission coordinates among notified bodies through sectoral groups of notified bodies.

EU-type examination certificates are valid for a maximum of five years, renewable after reassessment. Notified bodies can use sub-contractors and subsidiaries with client consent and adequate supervision.

Chapter VMarket Surveillance and Enforcement
Art. 52–54

Market Surveillance

Market surveillance follows the existing EU market surveillance framework (Regulation (EU) 2019/1020). Each member state designates one or more market surveillance authorities with adequate resources and cybersecurity expertise. The designated CSIRT for each member state supports market surveillance by providing technical expertise and cooperating on vulnerability assessments.

National market surveillance authorities can conduct simultaneous coordinated activities ("sweeps") to check compliance. Authorities must inform the Commission, other member states, and ENISA if they find that a product is non-compliant or poses a significant cybersecurity risk. ADCO (the Administrative Cooperation Group) coordinates enforcement priorities and joint actions.

Art. 55–58

Safeguard and Union Procedures

Where a market surveillance authority finds a non-compliant product, it requires the relevant economic operator to take corrective action within a specified time, and may restrict, prohibit, or withdraw the product. If non-compliance persists, the authority takes all appropriate measures including prohibiting or restricting market availability and ensuring withdrawal or recall.

Compliant products that nonetheless present a significant cybersecurity risk can be required to be modified, withdrawn, or recalled under a separate safeguard procedure. The Commission evaluates national actions and may determine whether they are justified, triggering EU-wide requirements. Formal non-compliance — missing CE marking, incorrect declarations, missing documentation — triggers a separate correction procedure.

Where market surveillance authorities have reason to believe a product presents a significant cybersecurity risk, they may carry out an evaluation covering all the essential requirements. The evaluation may be triggered by information from national CSIRTs, ENISA, or other member states. Authorities can require the manufacturer to take corrective actions within a specified timeframe.

Chapter VIDelegated Acts and Committee Procedure
Art. 61–62

Delegated Acts and Implementing Acts

The Commission has power to adopt delegated acts to: update the essential cybersecurity requirements in Annex I; update the information and instructions to users in Annex II; update the product categories in Annexes III and IV; specify which products require European cybersecurity certification; set minimum support periods; and specify the format of the software bill of materials. The delegation is granted for five years (renewable), revocable by Parliament or Council.

Implementing acts follow the committee examination procedure and cover: common specifications, technical specifications for reporting, and technical descriptions of Annex III and IV product categories.

Chapter VIIConfidentiality and Penalties
Art. 63

Confidentiality

All parties must keep confidential information and data obtained in carrying out their tasks, particularly to protect intellectual property rights, confidential business information, and trade secrets. The obligation does not prevent sharing of information with authorities or with ENISA for the purpose of applying this Regulation.

Art. 64

Penalties

Non-compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirements (Annex I) or with manufacturer obligations (Articles 13 and 14) — up to €15 million or 2.5% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Non-compliance with any other obligation under this Regulation — up to €10 million or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Supplying incorrect, incomplete, or misleading information to notified bodies or market surveillance authorities — up to €5 million or 1% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Member states set the penalty rules and must ensure they are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. When setting the amount, account must be taken of: the nature, gravity, duration, and scope of the infringement; actions taken to mitigate; previous infringements; the size and market share of the operator; and any other aggravating or mitigating factors. Small enterprises and microenterprises get proportionate treatment. Penalties must take into account the interests of SMEs including startups. Penalties under this Regulation and Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act) must not exceed the higher of the amounts set out in either Regulation for the same breach.

Chapter VIIITransitional and Final Provisions
Art. 65–68

EUVD, Review, and Transition

ENISA must establish and maintain a European Vulnerability Database (EUVD) — a publicly accessible, machine-readable database of known vulnerabilities in products with digital elements, including affected products, severity, exploitation status, available patches, and, where available, guidance on mitigation. Anyone may report a vulnerability to ENISA for inclusion. After receiving a report, ENISA must ensure that the affected manufacturer is informed. An early publication mechanism exists where public awareness is needed.

The Commission must evaluate and review this Regulation by 11 December 2030 and every four years thereafter, reporting to Parliament and Council — including on whether to extend scope, and whether a single EU cybersecurity authority is needed.

Products placed on the market before the essential requirements apply (before 11 December 2027) are only subject to this Regulation if they undergo a substantial modification after that date. However, manufacturers' reporting obligations (Art. 14) and ENISA's platform obligations apply from 11 September 2026. Conformity assessment body notification requirements apply from 11 June 2026.

Art. 71

Entry Into Force and Application

This Regulation entered into force on 10 December 2024. Conformity assessment body rules (Chapter IV) apply from 11 June 2026. Reporting obligations (Art. 14) apply from 11 September 2026. All remaining provisions — including the essential cybersecurity requirements, manufacturer obligations, market surveillance, and penalties — apply from 11 December 2027.
