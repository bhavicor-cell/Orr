Chapter IGeneral Provisions
Art. 1

Subject Matter

This Regulation sets out rules for protecting people when their personal data is processed, and rules for the free movement of personal data. It protects the fundamental right to data protection. Personal data must move freely within the EU — member states cannot restrict it on data-protection grounds.

Art. 2

Material Scope

This Regulation applies to the processing of personal data by automated means (wholly or partly), and to non-automated processing if the data forms part of a filing system or is intended to.

It does not apply to: processing outside the scope of EU law; member state activities under Common Foreign and Security Policy; purely personal or household activities; or processing by authorities for criminal law enforcement (which is covered by a separate Directive).

Art. 3

Territorial Scope

This Regulation applies to processing in the context of an establishment of a controller or processor in the EU — regardless of whether the processing itself happens inside the EU.

It also applies to controllers or processors not established in the EU, if they process data of people who are in the EU and the processing relates to: offering them goods or services (paid or free), or monitoring their behaviour within the EU.

It also applies where a controller outside the EU is in a place where member state law applies by virtue of public international law.

Art. 4

Definitions — selected key terms

Personal data — any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. A person is identifiable if they can be identified, directly or indirectly, by reference to a name, ID number, location data, online identifier, or factors specific to their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

Processing — any operation on personal data, whether automated or not: collection, recording, organisation, structuring, storage, adaptation, alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure, dissemination, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, destruction.

Controller — whoever determines the purposes and means of processing. Can be a person, company, public authority, or other body. Where purposes and means are set by EU or member state law, the controller or criteria for nomination may be specified by that law.

Processor — whoever processes personal data on behalf of the controller.

Consent — any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes, by statement or clear affirmative action, signifying agreement to processing.

Personal data breach — a breach of security leading to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data.

Profiling — automated processing of personal data to evaluate personal aspects of a person, in particular to analyse or predict performance at work, economic situation, health, preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location, or movements.

Pseudonymisation — processing personal data so it can no longer be attributed to a specific person without additional information, provided that additional information is kept separately under technical and organisational measures.

Supervisory authority — an independent public authority established by a member state to monitor application of this Regulation.

Main establishment — for a controller with establishments in multiple member states, the place of central administration — unless decisions on processing purposes and means are taken at another establishment that has power to implement them, in which case that establishment is the main one.

Binding corporate rules — internal data protection policies for transferring personal data within a group of undertakings to controllers or processors in third countries.

Chapter IIPrinciples
Art. 5

Principles Relating to Processing

Personal data must be: processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently; collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not used in a way incompatible with those purposes; adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary (data minimisation); accurate and kept up to date; kept in identifiable form no longer than necessary (storage limitation); and processed securely, with protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction, or damage (integrity and confidentiality).

The controller is responsible for complying with all of these and must be able to demonstrate compliance. This is the accountability principle.

Art. 6

Lawfulness of Processing

Processing is lawful only if at least one of the following applies: the data subject consented for one or more specific purposes; processing is necessary for performing a contract with the data subject or taking pre-contractual steps at their request; processing is necessary to comply with a legal obligation the controller is subject to; processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or another person; processing is necessary for a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority; or processing is necessary for legitimate interests of the controller or a third party, unless overridden by the data subject's interests or fundamental rights — particularly where the data subject is a child.

The legitimate-interests ground does not apply to public authorities performing their tasks.

If a controller wants to process for a new purpose beyond the original one, and neither consent nor law authorises it, the controller must assess compatibility by considering: the link between purposes; the context of collection; the nature of the data (including whether it is special-category or criminal-offence data); the possible consequences for the data subject; and whether appropriate safeguards exist.

Art. 7

Conditions for Consent

Where processing is based on consent, the controller must be able to prove it. If consent is bundled into a written declaration covering other matters, the consent request must be clearly distinguishable, intelligible, easily accessible, and in plain language. Any part that infringes this Regulation is not binding.

The data subject can withdraw consent at any time. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent. Withdrawal does not affect the lawfulness of processing done before it. The data subject must be told this before giving consent.

When assessing whether consent is freely given, you must consider whether performing a contract is made conditional on consent to processing that is not necessary for that contract. If it is, the consent is not freely given.

Art. 8

Children's Consent for Online Services

Where consent is the legal basis and the service is offered directly to a child, processing is lawful if the child is at least 16 — or a lower age set by the member state, but never below 13. Below that age, consent must be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility. The controller must make reasonable efforts to verify this.

Art. 9

Special Categories of Personal Data

Processing of data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data for identification, health data, or data about sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited — unless one of ten exceptions applies.

The exceptions include: explicit consent (unless member state law says consent cannot lift the prohibition); employment and social security obligations; vital interests where the person cannot consent; legitimate activities of a not-for-profit body with appropriate safeguards; data the person has manifestly made public; establishment, exercise, or defence of legal claims; substantial public interest under EU or member state law; preventive or occupational medicine, health or social care; public health reasons including cross-border health threats; and archiving, research, or statistical purposes.

Member states can introduce further conditions or limitations on genetic, biometric, or health data.

Art. 10

Criminal Conviction Data

Personal data relating to criminal convictions and offences may only be processed under the control of official authority, or when authorised by EU or member state law with appropriate safeguards. No comprehensive criminal-conviction register may be kept outside official authority.

Art. 11

Processing Not Requiring Identification

If the controller's purposes do not require identifying the data subject, the controller does not need to maintain or acquire additional information just to comply with this Regulation. In that case, the data-subject rights in Articles 15–20 do not apply — unless the data subject provides additional information to enable identification.

Chapter IIIRights of the Data Subject
Art. 12

Transparency and Modalities

All information and communication about processing must be concise, transparent, intelligible, easily accessible, and in clear, plain language — especially when addressed to a child. Information is provided in writing or electronically. The controller must facilitate the exercise of rights and respond within one month (extendable by two months for complex requests). If the controller does not act, it must explain why within one month. Requests are free of charge, unless manifestly unfounded or excessive.

Art. 13–14

Information Duties

When collecting personal data directly from the data subject, the controller must provide at the time of collection: its identity and contact details; the DPO's contact details; the purposes and legal basis of processing; legitimate interests relied upon; recipients; any intended cross-border transfer and its safeguards; the storage period or criteria for determining it; the existence of data-subject rights; the right to withdraw consent; the right to complain to a supervisory authority; whether providing data is a statutory or contractual requirement; and the existence of automated decision-making including profiling.

When data is not obtained from the data subject, the same information must be provided within a reasonable period (at most one month), or at first contact, or at first disclosure to another recipient. The controller must also state the categories of data and the source. Exceptions apply where the data subject already has the information, providing it is impossible or disproportionate, obtaining or disclosure is required by law, or the data must remain confidential under a professional secrecy obligation.

Art. 15

Right of Access

The data subject can ask the controller to confirm whether their data is being processed and, if so, get access to the data along with: the purposes; categories of data; recipients; the storage period or criteria; the existence of rights to rectification, erasure, restriction, and objection; the right to complain; the source of data not collected from the data subject; and the existence of automated decision-making. The controller must provide a copy of the data free of charge (reasonable fee for further copies).

Art. 16

Right to Rectification

The data subject can require the controller to correct inaccurate personal data without undue delay and to complete incomplete data.

Art. 17

Right to Erasure ("Right to Be Forgotten")

The data subject can require erasure where: the data is no longer necessary for its original purpose; consent is withdrawn and no other legal basis exists; the data subject objects under Article 21 and there are no overriding grounds; the data was unlawfully processed; erasure is required by EU or member state law; or the data was collected from a child in relation to an online service.

If the controller has made the data public, it must take reasonable steps to inform other controllers processing it that erasure has been requested.

Erasure does not apply where processing is necessary for: freedom of expression and information; compliance with a legal obligation or a public-interest or official-authority task; public health reasons; archiving, research, or statistical purposes; or establishing, exercising, or defending legal claims.

Art. 18

Right to Restriction of Processing

The data subject can require the controller to restrict processing where: accuracy is contested (while verifying); processing is unlawful but the data subject prefers restriction over erasure; the controller no longer needs the data but the data subject needs it for legal claims; or the data subject has objected under Article 21 (pending verification of whether the controller's grounds override). Restricted data may only be processed (beyond storage) with the data subject's consent, for legal claims, to protect another's rights, or for important public interest.

Art. 19

Notification of Rectification, Erasure, or Restriction

The controller must tell each recipient of rectification, erasure, or restriction — unless impossible or disproportionate — and must tell the data subject who those recipients are, if asked.

Art. 20

Right to Data Portability

Where processing is based on consent or a contract and carried out by automated means, the data subject has the right to receive their personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and transmit it to another controller without hindrance. Where technically feasible, the data subject can require direct transmission between controllers. This right does not apply to public-interest or official-authority processing and must not adversely affect others' rights.

Art. 21

Right to Object

The data subject can object at any time to processing based on public interest, official authority, or legitimate interests (including profiling). The controller must stop unless it demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds that override the data subject's interests, rights, and freedoms, or the processing is for legal claims.

For direct marketing, the right to object is absolute — no balancing test. Once the data subject objects, the data may no longer be processed for marketing. This right must be brought to the data subject's attention clearly and separately at first contact.

Art. 22

Automated Decision-Making and Profiling

The data subject has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing (including profiling) that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them — unless the decision is necessary for a contract, authorised by EU or member state law with safeguards, or based on explicit consent. In the contract and consent cases, the controller must implement suitable measures to safeguard rights, including at minimum the right to human intervention, to express a point of view, and to contest the decision. Automated decisions may not be based on special-category data unless explicit consent or substantial public interest applies, with suitable safeguards.

Art. 23

Restrictions

EU or member state law can restrict the scope of data-subject rights and controller obligations (Articles 5 and 12–22) where necessary and proportionate to safeguard: national security; defence; public security; criminal law enforcement; other important public interests; judicial independence; professional ethics enforcement; regulatory functions; protection of the data subject or others' rights; or enforcement of civil law claims. Any such restriction must respect the essence of fundamental rights and contain specific provisions on purposes, data categories, scope, safeguards, storage periods, risks, and the data subject's right to be informed.

Chapter IVController and Processor
Art. 24–25

Responsibility and Data Protection by Design

The controller must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure and demonstrate that processing complies with this Regulation, taking into account the nature, scope, context, purposes, and risk. These measures must be reviewed and updated as needed. Adherence to approved codes of conduct or certification mechanisms can help demonstrate compliance.

Data protection by design: the controller must build data-protection principles (like data minimisation and pseudonymisation) into the design of processing, both when determining the means and during processing itself. Data protection by default: only personal data necessary for each specific purpose is processed by default — covering amount collected, extent of processing, storage period, and accessibility. Data must not be accessible to an indefinite number of people without the individual's intervention.

Art. 26

Joint Controllers

Where two or more controllers jointly determine purposes and means, they must transparently arrange their respective responsibilities — particularly for data-subject rights and information duties. The arrangement's essence must be made available to data subjects. Regardless of the arrangement, a data subject can exercise rights against any of the controllers.

Art. 27

Representatives

Controllers or processors not established in the EU but caught by Article 3(2) must designate a written representative in the EU — except where processing is occasional, small-scale, low-risk, or the controller is a public authority.

Art. 28

Processor

A controller may only use processors that provide sufficient guarantees of appropriate technical and organisational measures. The processor cannot engage a sub-processor without the controller's prior written authorisation (specific or general). Processing must be governed by a binding contract or legal act specifying: subject-matter, duration, nature, purpose, data type, data-subject categories, and the controller's obligations and rights.

The contract must require the processor to: process only on the controller's documented instructions; ensure confidentiality; implement security measures; respect sub-processing conditions; assist with data-subject rights requests; assist with security, breach notification, impact assessments, and prior consultation; delete or return all data after the service ends; and make all compliance information available for audits. If a processor determines purposes and means itself, it becomes a controller for that processing.

Art. 29–31

Processing Under Authority, Records, Cooperation

No one acting under the controller's or processor's authority may process personal data except on the controller's instructions, unless required by law.

Both controllers and processors must maintain records of processing activities, in writing (including electronic form), available to the supervisory authority on request. Records must include: names and contact details; purposes; data-subject and data categories; recipients; transfers; retention periods; and a description of security measures. The record-keeping obligation does not apply to organisations with fewer than 250 employees — unless the processing is risky, not occasional, or involves special-category or criminal-offence data.

Controllers and processors must cooperate with the supervisory authority on request.

Art. 32

Security of Processing

Controllers and processors must implement security measures appropriate to the risk, including: pseudonymisation and encryption; ensuring ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience; the ability to restore access after an incident; and regular testing and evaluation of security measures.

Art. 33–34

Breach Notification

When a personal data breach occurs, the controller must notify the supervisory authority without undue delay — within 72 hours where feasible — unless the breach is unlikely to risk anyone's rights or freedoms. Late notifications must include the reasons for delay. The processor must notify the controller without undue delay. The notification must describe: the nature of the breach (categories and approximate numbers); the DPO or contact point; likely consequences; and measures taken or proposed to address it. Information can be provided in phases.

If the breach is likely to result in high risk to people's rights and freedoms, the controller must also communicate it to the affected data subjects without undue delay, in clear, plain language. This communication is not required if: the data was encrypted or otherwise unintelligible to unauthorised persons; subsequent measures eliminated the high risk; or it would involve disproportionate effort (in which case a public communication must be used instead).

Art. 35–36

Data Protection Impact Assessment and Prior Consultation

Where processing is likely to result in high risk — especially using new technologies — the controller must carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment before processing. A DPIA is required in particular for: systematic, extensive automated evaluation of personal aspects (profiling) on which decisions with legal or significant effects are based; large-scale processing of special-category or criminal-offence data; and systematic large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas.

The assessment must contain: a systematic description of the processing and its purposes; an assessment of necessity and proportionality; an assessment of the risks to data subjects; and the measures envisaged to address those risks.

Where the DPIA indicates high risk that the controller cannot mitigate, the controller must consult the supervisory authority before processing. The authority has up to eight weeks (extendable by six) to provide written advice.

Art. 37–39

Data Protection Officer

A DPO must be designated where: the controller or processor is a public authority (except courts in their judicial capacity); their core activities require regular and systematic large-scale monitoring of data subjects; or their core activities involve large-scale processing of special-category or criminal-offence data.

The DPO is designated based on professional qualities and expert knowledge of data protection law. The DPO can be a staff member or external. The DPO must be involved in all data-protection issues properly and in a timely manner, given necessary resources, must not receive instructions regarding their tasks, cannot be dismissed or penalised for performing them, and reports directly to the highest management level. Data subjects can contact the DPO directly.

The DPO's minimum tasks are: informing and advising the controller or processor and staff; monitoring compliance; advising on DPIAs; cooperating with the supervisory authority; and acting as the contact point for the authority.

Art. 40–43

Codes of Conduct and Certification

Industry associations can draw up codes of conduct specifying how this Regulation applies in their sector. Codes must include mandatory compliance-monitoring mechanisms, are submitted to the supervisory authority for approval, and can be given EU-wide general validity by the Commission.

Data protection certification mechanisms, seals, and marks can be established to demonstrate compliance. Certification is voluntary, issued for a maximum of three years (renewable), and does not reduce the controller's or processor's responsibility. Certification bodies must be accredited by the supervisory authority or national accreditation body.

Chapter VTransfers to Third Countries
Art. 44

General Principle

Any transfer of personal data to a third country or international organisation may only happen if the conditions in this Chapter are met — by the controller and processor, including for onward transfers. The level of protection guaranteed by this Regulation must not be undermined.

Art. 45

Adequacy Decisions

The Commission can decide that a third country, territory, sector, or international organisation ensures an adequate level of protection. Transfers to that destination then require no specific authorisation. The Commission assesses: rule of law, human rights, relevant legislation, data protection rules, enforceable data-subject rights, effective judicial redress, independent supervisory authorities, and international commitments. Adequacy decisions are reviewed at least every four years and can be repealed, amended, or suspended.

Art. 46

Appropriate Safeguards

Without an adequacy decision, transfers can happen if the controller or processor provides appropriate safeguards with enforceable data-subject rights and effective legal remedies. Safeguards that do not need specific supervisory authority authorisation include: legally binding instruments between public bodies; binding corporate rules; standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commission; standard clauses adopted by a supervisory authority and approved by the Commission; approved codes of conduct with binding commitments; and approved certification mechanisms with binding commitments.

Art. 47

Binding Corporate Rules

Binding corporate rules for intra-group transfers must be legally binding on every group member, expressly confer enforceable rights on data subjects, and meet detailed requirements: structure and contacts, transfer details, legal bindingness, application of data-protection principles, data-subject rights, liability acceptance by the EU-established member, information provision, DPO tasks, complaint procedures, compliance verification, change reporting, supervisory authority cooperation, third-country legal-requirement reporting, and staff training.

Art. 48–49

Unauthorised Transfers and Derogations

A third-country court or authority judgment requiring transfer or disclosure is only recognised or enforceable if based on an international agreement like a mutual legal assistance treaty.

Without an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards, a transfer may still happen if: the data subject explicitly consented after being informed of the risks; the transfer is necessary for a contract with the data subject; the transfer is necessary for a contract in the data subject's interest; it is necessary for important public interest; it is necessary for legal claims; it is necessary to protect vital interests where the person cannot consent; or the transfer is from a legally required public register. As a last resort, non-repetitive transfers affecting a limited number of data subjects may proceed for the controller's compelling legitimate interests, with safeguards, supervisory authority notification, and data-subject information.

Chapter VISupervisory Authorities
Art. 51–54

Independence, Competence, Establishment

Each member state must establish one or more independent supervisory authorities to monitor and enforce this Regulation. Supervisory authorities must act with complete independence — free from external influence, seeking no instructions from anyone. Member states must provide adequate human, technical, financial resources, premises, and infrastructure. Members are appointed transparently, must have data-protection qualifications, serve terms of at least four years, and can only be dismissed for serious misconduct or failure to meet required conditions.

Art. 55–59

Competence, Tasks, and Powers

Each authority is competent on its own territory. For cross-border processing, the authority of the controller's or processor's main establishment acts as lead supervisory authority.

Tasks include: monitoring and enforcing the Regulation; promoting public awareness; advising governments; promoting controller and processor awareness; handling complaints; cooperating with other authorities; investigating; monitoring relevant developments; and encouraging codes of conduct and certification.

Investigative powers: ordering controllers and processors to provide information; conducting data-protection audits; reviewing certifications; notifying of alleged infringements; obtaining access to data and premises. Corrective powers: issuing warnings and reprimands; ordering compliance, communication of breaches, rectification, erasure, or restriction; imposing processing bans; ordering suspension of data flows; imposing administrative fines. Advisory powers: advising on prior consultation, issuing opinions, approving codes of conduct, accrediting certification bodies, issuing certifications, approving binding corporate rules, and authorising contractual clauses. Each authority publishes an annual activity report.

Chapter VIICooperation and Consistency
Art. 60–67

Cooperation Between Authorities

The lead supervisory authority cooperates with other concerned authorities, sharing relevant information and seeking consensus on draft decisions. Where a concerned authority raises a relevant and reasoned objection to a draft decision, and the lead authority does not follow it, the matter goes to the consistency mechanism for resolution by the European Data Protection Board. Authorities must provide mutual assistance to each other — responding within one month — and may conduct joint operations including joint investigations.

The Board ensures consistent application. It issues opinions on supervisory authority draft measures (impact assessment lists, codes of conduct, certification criteria, standard clauses, binding corporate rules). It adopts binding decisions to resolve disputes between authorities. An urgency procedure allows provisional measures with a three-month validity, with urgent Board decisions adopted within two weeks.

Art. 68–76

European Data Protection Board

The Board is a body of the Union with legal personality, composed of the head of each member state's supervisory authority and the European Data Protection Supervisor. The Commission participates without voting rights. The Board acts independently — seeking no instructions from anyone.

The Board's tasks include: monitoring and ensuring correct application; advising the Commission; issuing guidelines, recommendations, and best practices on erasure, profiling, breach notification, data transfers, and administrative fines; encouraging codes of conduct and certification; carrying out accreditation; providing opinions on adequacy decisions and draft supervisory measures; and maintaining a public register of decisions.

The Board decides by simple majority (unless otherwise provided), elects a Chair and two deputies for five-year renewable terms, and is supported by a secretariat provided by the European Data Protection Supervisor.

Chapter VIIIRemedies, Liability, and Penalties
Art. 77–78

Right to Complain and Judicial Remedy Against Authorities

Every data subject can lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority — in the member state of their habitual residence, place of work, or place of the alleged infringement. The authority must inform the complainant of progress and outcome.

Every person has the right to an effective judicial remedy against a binding decision of a supervisory authority, and against an authority's failure to handle a complaint or inform the data subject within three months.

Art. 79

Judicial Remedy Against Controllers or Processors

Every data subject has the right to an effective judicial remedy where they consider their rights have been infringed by non-compliant processing. Proceedings are brought where the controller or processor has an establishment, or where the data subject has habitual residence — unless the controller or processor is a public authority acting in its public powers.

Art. 80

Representation

Data subjects can mandate a not-for-profit body active in data protection to lodge complaints, exercise rights, and (where member state law allows) seek compensation on their behalf. Member states may also allow such bodies to act independently of a data subject's mandate.

Art. 82

Compensation and Liability

Anyone who suffers material or non-material damage from an infringement has the right to compensation from the controller or processor. A controller is liable for damage caused by processing that infringes this Regulation. A processor is liable only for failing to comply with processor-specific obligations, or acting outside or contrary to the controller's lawful instructions. A controller or processor is exempt only if it proves it is not in any way responsible for the event causing the damage. Where multiple controllers or processors are involved, each is liable for the entire damage — with a right to claim back from the others in proportion to their responsibility.

Art. 83

Administrative Fines

Fines must be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each case. When setting fines, the authority considers: the nature, gravity, duration, and scope of the infringement; whether it was intentional or negligent; mitigation actions; technical and organisational measures; previous infringements; cooperation with the authority; categories of data affected; how the authority learned of the infringement; compliance with prior orders; adherence to codes of conduct or certification; and any financial benefit gained.

Lower tier (up to €10 million or 2% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher): breaches of controller and processor obligations under Articles 8, 11, 25–39, 42, and 43; certification body obligations; and monitoring body obligations.

Upper tier (up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher): breaches of the basic processing principles and consent conditions (Articles 5, 6, 7, 9); data-subject rights (Articles 12–22); cross-border transfer rules (Articles 44–49); member state law obligations under Chapter IX; and non-compliance with a supervisory authority order or processing ban.

Art. 84

Other Penalties

Member states must lay down rules on other penalties for infringements not subject to administrative fines, and ensure they are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.

Chapter IXSpecific Processing Situations
Art. 85–91

Special Rules

Member states must reconcile data protection with freedom of expression and information, providing exemptions or derogations for journalistic, academic, artistic, or literary purposes.

Personal data in official documents held by public authorities may be disclosed to reconcile public access with data protection.

Member states may set specific conditions for processing national identification numbers.

Member states may provide more specific rules for employee data processing in the employment context, including for recruitment, performance, health and safety, and termination — with suitable safeguards for human dignity, legitimate interests, and fundamental rights.

Processing for archiving, research, or statistical purposes must include appropriate safeguards, in particular data minimisation and pseudonymisation. Member state law may derogate from certain data-subject rights for these purposes where the rights would render the purpose impossible or seriously impair it.

Member states may adopt specific rules on supervisory authority investigative powers in relation to controllers or processors subject to professional secrecy obligations.

Existing comprehensive data protection rules of churches and religious associations may continue to apply, provided they are brought into line with this Regulation, under the supervision of an independent supervisory authority.

Chapter X–XIDelegated Acts and Final Provisions
Art. 92–93

Delegated and Implementing Acts

The Commission has power to adopt delegated acts (on standardised icons and certification requirements) for an indeterminate period, revocable by Parliament or Council at any time. A three-month objection period applies. Implementing acts follow the committee examination procedure.

Art. 94–96

Repeal and Relationship with Other Instruments

Directive 95/46/EC (the predecessor data protection directive) was repealed on 25 May 2018. References to the old Directive are read as references to this Regulation. This Regulation does not impose additional obligations where specific obligations with the same objective already exist under the ePrivacy Directive (2002/58/EC). International agreements involving personal data transfers concluded before 24 May 2016 remain in force until amended, replaced, or revoked.

Art. 97

Commission Reports

The Commission reports on the evaluation and review of this Regulation to the European Parliament and Council every four years (first report was due by 25 May 2020), examining in particular cross-border transfer rules and the cooperation and consistency mechanism.

Art. 99

Entry into Force

This Regulation entered into force on 24 May 2016 and applies from 25 May 2018. It is binding in its entirety and directly applicable in all member states.
