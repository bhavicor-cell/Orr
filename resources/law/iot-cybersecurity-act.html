Definitions
Sec. 2

Definitions

Agency — any executive agency as defined in the existing US Code (5 U.S.C. §105), but not including the Department of Defense, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, or any element of the intelligence community.

Covered device (Internet of Things device) — a device that has at least one transducer (sensor or actuator) for interacting directly with the physical world, has at least one network interface (which may include Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, Zigbee, or similar), and is not a conventional IT device — meaning it is not a general-purpose computing device like a smartphone, laptop, desktop, server, tablet, or mainframe where the primary function is computing or data processing rather than sensing or acting on the physical world.

Security vulnerability — any attribute of hardware, software, process, or procedure that could enable or facilitate the defeat of a security control.

NIST — the National Institute of Standards and Technology.

NIST Standards and Guidelines
Sec. 3

NIST IoT Security Standards

Within 90 days of enactment, NIST must publish standards and guidelines for the federal government on the appropriate use and management of IoT devices owned or controlled by an agency and connected to agency information systems — including minimum information security requirements for managing cybersecurity risks associated with those devices.

The standards must address at minimum: secure development, identity management, patching, and configuration management for IoT devices. They must be consistent with the existing NIST framework for federal information security (FISMA) and align with NIST's other information security standards and guidelines.

NIST must review the standards at least every five years and revise them as appropriate. In developing and reviewing, NIST must consult with cybersecurity researchers, relevant agencies, private-sector experts, and the public.

Sec. 5

IoT Cybersecurity Guidelines for Federal Agencies

Within 180 days after NIST publishes its standards and guidelines, the Director of OMB must review agency information security policies and principles to ensure they are consistent with NIST's IoT guidelines. Where necessary, the Director must issue policies, principles, standards, or guidelines that address IoT security in a manner consistent with NIST's work.

Federal Procurement
Sec. 4

IoT Cybersecurity for Federal Agencies

After NIST publishes its standards and guidelines, agencies may not procure, obtain, or use an IoT device if the Chief Information Officer of that agency determines, during a review of a contract for such a device, that the use of the device prevents compliance with the NIST standards and guidelines.

This procurement restriction applies to new contracts and contract renewals, not to existing contracts that are not up for renewal.

Sec. 8

Implementation, Enforcement, and Waivers

Agencies must implement the NIST standards and guidelines for IoT devices. An agency may waive the procurement restriction only if: the waiver is necessary in the interest of national security; the waiver is needed for the purpose of research; or the device is secured using alternative effective methods — which the Chief Information Officer must document in writing.

The waiver documentation must include: the particular device or class of devices covered; the justification for the waiver; the duration (not exceeding two years, unless renewed); the alternative mitigation actions in place; and a determination that the waiver is in the interest of the agency. The CIO must provide waiver documentation to the Director of OMB on a quarterly basis.

Vulnerability Disclosure
Sec. 6

Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure by Contractors and Vendors

Within two years of enactment, the Director of OMB must develop and oversee the implementation of policies, principles, standards, or guidelines as necessary to address security vulnerabilities in information systems (including IoT devices). These policies must be consistent with NIST's vulnerability disclosure guidelines (Sec. 7) and must require each agency's CIO to develop procedures for coordinated vulnerability disclosure — covering both IoT devices and conventional IT equipment used by the agency.

Contractors and vendors providing information technology (including IoT devices) to federal agencies must: adopt coordinated vulnerability disclosure policies and procedures consistent with NIST guidelines; and have the ability to receive, manage, and resolve security vulnerability reports in a timely manner.

Sec. 7

NIST Vulnerability Disclosure Guidelines

NIST must publish guidelines for: receiving information about a potential security vulnerability relating to agency information systems (including IoT devices); disseminating that information to the relevant entity responsible for remediation; publishing information about resolved security vulnerabilities, including through the National Vulnerability Database; and procedures for coordinated vulnerability disclosure, taking into account ISO standards (29147 and 30111).

NIST must consult with cybersecurity researchers, the private sector, and relevant agencies in developing these guidelines, and must review them periodically.

Oversight and Limitations
Sec. 9

GAO Report

The Comptroller General of the United States must submit a report to Congress within two years of enactment covering: the effectiveness of the NIST standards and guidelines; agency compliance with the procurement restrictions; the effectiveness of coordinated vulnerability disclosure practices across agencies; and any recommendations for improving federal IoT cybersecurity.

Sec. 10

Limitations on Definitions

The definitions in this Act apply only for the purposes of this Act. NIST may not use the definitions in this Act as the basis for any binding cybersecurity standard or guideline that applies to private-sector entities (outside the federal government). Nothing in this Act authorises any federal agency to regulate the cybersecurity practices of non-federal entities.

Sec. 11

Authority

Nothing in this Act creates or expands the authority of any federal agency. If any provision is held invalid, the remainder of the Act continues in full force and effect.

Sec. 12

Savings Clause

Nothing in this Act or any amendment made by this Act affects any authority, duty, obligation, or liability of any entity under any other provision of law.

Sec. 13

Effective Date

This Act took effect on 4 December 2020.

This is a federal-procurement law. It sets cybersecurity requirements for IoT devices bought by the US government — it does not directly regulate private-sector IoT products. What it does do is shape the market: vendors who want to sell to the federal government must meet NIST standards and maintain coordinated vulnerability disclosure. Working out whether your product falls under “covered device” and which NIST standards apply — is a different exercise.
